# Project A.M.P.E.R.E.
## Automated Measurement of Parasitic Electrical Responses and Events
### System Design and Validation

## Abstract

Charge Circuit Interrupting Devices (CCIDs) are the ground-fault protective element inside electric vehicle supply equipment, responsible for detecting a leakage fault and interrupting power within a bounded time window. Verifying that behavior with confidence requires exercising a device across a large number of repeated fault cycles, capturing each response with enough timing precision to distinguish a passing trip from a marginal one, and doing so without a human manually operating switches or reading an oscilloscope thousands of times. Project A.M.P.E.R.E. is an automated test system built to perform exactly that: a Raspberry Pi-driven rig that repeatedly injects a controlled ground fault into a live EVSE, captures the resulting transient on a digital oscilloscope, verifies via machine vision that the fault was only ever injected while the EVSE was genuinely in a charging state, and records every cycle's raw waveform and verdict to a crash-safe, resumable data store. This report describes the system's electrical and software design, the measurement methodology used to convert a raw voltage waveform into a trip-time verdict, and the commissioning process that validated the rig before it was trusted to run unattended. It does not present the results of any specific test campaign; those are reported separately in Addendum I, which covers a 6,000-cycle endurance campaign run on this system.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Background and Motivation

A ground-fault circuit interrupter protecting electric vehicle charging equipment has one job that matters above all others: when a leakage fault occurs, it must interrupt the circuit before the fault can do harm, and it must do so within a bounded amount of time. UL 2231-2 is the governing standard for this behavior, and it is written around the assumption that a device's trip-time can be measured precisely and repeatably. In practice, proving that a given device meets that bound is not a one-time measurement. A single trip-time reading says very little on its own; confidence comes from exercising the device across many repeated fault events and confirming that its response stays consistently within bounds, cycle after cycle, without drift, without an occasional slow response hiding in the tail of the distribution.

Doing that manually does not scale. Each cycle requires safely energizing the rig, injecting a fault at a known point in time, capturing a fast transient on an oscilloscope with the right trigger and timebase settings, reading the result, and safely de-energizing again before the next cycle. A human operator can do this a handful of times in a session; a meaningful reliability claim requires thousands of cycles, run back to back, over days. That gap between what a manual test bench can produce and what a real reliability claim requires is the reason this system exists: to make thousands of precisely-timed, safely-executed fault cycles achievable without a person standing at the bench for each one.

### 1.2 Objectives

The system was built to meet several requirements simultaneously, not just to inject a fault and read a number:

Repeatably and safely induce a controlled ground-fault condition on a live EVSE, using independently driven mains and leakage-injection contactors, with hardware and software interlocks that keep the rig in a known-safe de-energized state by default and that cannot be defeated by a single point of software failure.

Capture each fault event's electrical response with a real digital oscilloscope, at a timing resolution fine enough to distinguish a pass from a fail within a few tens of microseconds, and preserve the full raw waveform rather than only a computed number, so that the measurement can be re-analyzed later without repeating the physical test.

Guarantee that a fault is only ever injected while the EVSE under test is genuinely in a charging state, using an independent machine-vision check on the device's own status indicator, since injecting into an EVSE that is not actually charging would produce a meaningless result that looks identical to a real one.

Run unattended, for extended periods, recovering automatically from the kinds of transient failures a multi-day campaign will inevitably encounter, while treating a genuine device failure as something that must stop the run and reach a human, never something the system quietly retries away.

Preserve a complete, trustworthy, crash-safe record of every cycle, structured so that the recorded verdict can always be traced back to the raw data it came from, and so that a version of the analysis algorithm applied to historical data is always known and never silently overwritten.

### 1.3 Scope of This Report

This report describes the system as it was designed, built, and validated prior to any large-scale test campaign. Section 2 covers the physical and electrical architecture of the rig, the safety interlocks that govern it, the instrumentation used to capture a fault event, the software architecture that coordinates all of it, and the data-persistence design that makes the system crash-safe. Section 3 covers the measurement methodology in detail: how a trip-time is defined, how the analysis algorithm turns a raw waveform into a verdict, the algorithm's own versioned evolution, and how the charging-gate vision check works. Section 4 covers commissioning: the validation steps taken before the rig was trusted with real hardware, the real debugging investigations that shaped the current design, and the limitations that were still open going into the system's first large endurance campaign.

This report does not cover the execution or results of any specific test campaign. The 6,000-cycle endurance campaign run on this system, including its operational timeline, incidents encountered during the run, and the resulting trip-time data and analysis, is documented separately in Addendum I. This report also does not constitute a UL 2231-2 certification record; the measurement endpoint definition used by the system is, as of this writing, the project's own provisional interpretation of the standard, pending confirmation against the published text, and that open item is stated plainly where it is relevant rather than glossed over.

## 2. System Design

### 2.1 Physical Test Rig Overview

The rig centers on a real EVSE under test, driven by three independently controlled contactors: K1 and K2 switch the L1 and L2 mains legs feeding the EVSE, and K3 switches a separate leakage-injection path that introduces the ground-fault current the CCID inside the EVSE is expected to detect and clear. All three are single-pole contactors, each driven by its own ZX-517 dual-MOSFET opto-driver board from its own isolated 12 VDC supply, and each commanded independently by a Raspberry Pi GPIO line (K1 on GPIO17, K2 on GPIO27, K3 on GPIO22). Because the EVSE needs to believe a vehicle is actually present and requesting a charge before it will energize, the rig uses a static control-pilot spoof device rather than a physical vehicle, so a full charge cycle can be exercised without an EV connected.

Two independent sensing paths observe the rig during a cycle. A Keysight digital oscilloscope is connected across the measurement node to capture the electrical transient produced by each fault injection, and a USB webcam is aimed at the EVSE's own status LED to confirm, optically, that the EVSE has actually reached a charging state before a fault is ever injected. Neither sensing path is treated as a stand-in for the other: the scope is the only source of a trip-time measurement, and the camera is the only source of charging-state confirmation, and the system never lets one substitute for the other.

### 2.2 Electrical Design and Safety Interlocks

The single highest-priority hazard in the electrical design is the driver boards themselves: the ZX-517 boards used to switch the contactor coils have no onboard flyback protection. A MOSFET switching an inductive coil without flyback suppression avalanche-stresses on turn-off and typically fails shorted, and a shorted K3 driver means the leakage-injection contactor is stuck permanently closed - defeating the gate pulldowns, the normally-open contactor behavior, and any software-side timing backstop simultaneously, with no software remedy once it has happened. Every coil in the system therefore has its own external flyback diode across it, sized with headroom over the minimum rating, oriented so a reversed installation would be an immediately visible dead short rather than a silent latent fault. Each MOSFET gate also carries its own pulldown resistor, so that if the Pi reboots and its GPIO lines revert to a floating input state, every contactor defaults open rather than floating into an undefined state. All three driver boards share a single ground bond back to the Pi, since the boards are not optoisolated and their MOSFET gates reference supply negative rather than Pi ground without that bond in place.

On the software side, every GPIO output is initialized inactive and driven active-high, so the rig's default state - power not yet commanded, or GPIO not yet under program control at all - is de-energized. The system's own default assumption throughout is that a contactor is open unless something has actively and recently commanded it closed; nothing about the design tries to infer physical contactor state from anything other than what was last commanded, and the system does not currently have auxiliary-contact or voltage feedback confirming that a commanded state was actually achieved - this is treated as a known, deliberate gap rather than a hidden one, and is addressed further in Section 4.5.

The leakage-injection path carries an additional, purely software-enforced safety measure: a 300-millisecond hard backstop on how long K3 is ever allowed to remain closed within a single cycle, independent of whether the oscilloscope ever reports a successful acquisition. This backstop is enforced by polling the elapsed time in short slices rather than issuing one long blocking wait, specifically so that a slow or stalled acquisition on the instrumentation side can never be allowed to hold K3 closed past its deadline. Closing K3 at all is itself gated on a single-use, per-cycle authorization token that is only issued once the charging-gate vision check (Section 3.4) has confirmed the EVSE is genuinely charging - a fault is never injected into a rig that has not been independently confirmed to be in the state the test assumes it is in.

### 2.3 Instrumentation

Trip-time measurement is performed with a Keysight MSO-X 2014A digital oscilloscope, connected to the Raspberry Pi over USB and driven entirely through a pure-Python VISA stack (PyVISA, PyVISA-py, and PyUSB), since no ARM build of a vendor VISA runtime exists for this platform. The scope is configured for a single, edge-triggered acquisition per cycle: channel 1 is the measurement input through a 10x, 300 V CAT II passive probe, the trigger is a positive edge at +20 V with trigger coupling set to DC and noise-reject disabled, and each acquisition captures a full one-million-point record at 50 ms/div with a centered timebase. That timebase is deliberately generous relative to the pass/fail limits the system evaluates against, so that a record is always long enough to either observe the current collapse directly or conclusively rule out that a trip occurred within the allowed window, rather than leaving that judgment ambiguous. The full-depth, one-million-point capture was a deliberate choice over a coarser, faster-to-transfer record; a fallback to a shorter record was considered during design but has never actually been needed in practice.

Charging-state confirmation is performed with a Logitech C270 USB webcam, mechanically aimed at the EVSE's own status LED and read through a calibrated region of interest rather than the full frame. The camera's exposure is fixed and manually locked rather than left on automatic exposure, since automatic exposure and automatic white balance both proved unreliable for consistent color classification of the LED and are actively worked around rather than relied upon. The camera's own device path is resolved through a udev rule keyed to the camera's USB serial number rather than to whatever `/dev/videoN` index the kernel happens to assign it, since a raw numeric index is not stable across USB reconnects and a real mid-campaign re-enumeration event is part of why this design choice exists (Section 4.4).

All contactor switching is performed through the Raspberry Pi's native GPIO, via the `gpiozero` library running on its `lgpio` backend.

### 2.4 Software Architecture

The system's control software is organized around a single per-cycle state machine, referred to throughout as the sequencer, that walks a cycle through a fixed sequence of states - from a safe, fully de-energized starting state, through closing the mains contactors and waiting for the vision system to confirm a genuine charging state, through configuring and arming the oscilloscope, through the leakage injection itself, and back to a fully de-energized state again - regardless of whether the cycle ultimately produces a pass, a fail, or a halt. Every hardware subsystem the sequencer depends on - the contactors, the oscilloscope, and the camera - sits behind a hardware abstraction layer with two interchangeable implementations: a real driver that talks to actual hardware, and a simulated driver that reproduces the same interface and the same class of failure behavior in software only. Which mode each subsystem runs in is an independent setting in the system's configuration file, which is what allowed the software itself - the state machine, the safety interlocks, the persistence and resume logic, the retry behavior - to be exercised by an extensive, fully deterministic automated test suite that runs in seconds and touches no real hardware at all, and what allowed individual subsystems to be switched from simulated to real one at a time during commissioning rather than all at once.

Configuration itself is loaded from a single YAML file, validated strictly against an explicit set of recognized keys - an unrecognized key anywhere in the file fails loudly at load time rather than being silently ignored - and reduced to a canonical hash that is frozen into every run's own record at the moment that run starts. That hash is what lets the system refuse to resume a run whose configuration has silently changed since it began, and lets a stored measurement always be traced back to the exact set of thresholds and settings that produced it.

### 2.5 Data Persistence and Crash Safety

Every cycle's data is committed to disk in a fixed order, chosen specifically so that a crash at any point during a cycle leaves the run in a state that can always be cleanly recovered on the next start, without ever losing a completed cycle or double-counting one that never actually finished. The raw waveform, the scope's own screenshot, the camera's gate-decision frame, and a per-cycle JSON sidecar describing the full state-transition history and analysis result are all written and flushed to disk first; only after all of that exists does the system append the cycle's row to the run's CSV log; only after that succeeds does it atomically replace the run's single state file with an updated one recording the new highest completed cycle number; and only after that file update is durable does the system send an external liveness signal. A resume operation trusts nothing but that state file's own record of the highest completed cycle number, and any artifact left behind by a cycle that never reached that point is treated as an orphan and discarded rather than trusted.

Two further properties of this design are deliberate rather than incidental. First, a halted run stays halted: the run's own state file records why it stopped, and that halt is sticky across restarts until it is explicitly overridden, so a device that has already failed a real test cannot be silently re-tested without a human being aware that a halt occurred. Second, every raw waveform is preserved in full, not just the number the analysis algorithm computed from it at the time, specifically so that a later, corrected, or simply different version of the analysis algorithm can be applied to historical data without repeating the physical test - a property that turned out to matter in practice, and is discussed further in Section 3.3.

## 3. Measurement Methodology

### 3.1 Trip-Time Definition and Measurement Endpoints

Trip time is defined as the elapsed time between the onset of the injected fault and the point at which the fault current collapses, and every measurement the system produces reduces, ultimately, to a pair of instants: an onset time, t0, and an end time, t_end, with trip_time_s computed as their difference. Neither instant is available for free from the instrumentation. The oscilloscope's own hardware trigger is deliberately never assumed to mark the true start of the fault, because the trigger level can fire up to roughly half a mains cycle after the fault current actually begins to flow; treating the trigger as t0 would systematically overstate every trip time by an amount that depends on line phase at the moment of injection, not on the device under test. Instead, t0 is resolved from the clearest available source in a fixed order of preference - an explicit injection timestamp if one is ever supplied, a timestamp embedded in the scope's own capture preamble if one is ever populated, and, in current practice, an onset detected directly from the shape of the captured waveform itself, which is the path every real measurement in this system actually takes today.

The specific definition of where t0 and t_end fall within that waveform is not an arbitrary implementation detail; it is the system's own working interpretation of the measurement endpoints described in UL 2231-2 §23.3.1, written out explicitly and frozen into the system's configuration so that it cannot drift silently between cycles or between campaigns. That definition remains provisional. It has not, as of this writing, been confirmed against the published standard text, and this report does not claim otherwise; the practical consequence is that the two numeric limits the system evaluates trip time against - a pass limit of 24.97 milliseconds and a no-trip limit of 100 milliseconds - should be understood as the thresholds this system currently applies, not as an independently certified interpretation of the standard.

### 3.2 The Analysis Algorithm

Converting one million raw samples into a single trip-time number is done by treating the waveform as an envelope problem rather than a simple threshold-crossing problem on the raw signal. The absolute value of the waveform is smoothed into two complementary sliding-window envelopes computed over roughly half a mains cycle - one that looks forward from each sample and one that looks backward - because a raw AC signal crosses zero twice every cycle even while a fault is fully conducting, and a naive detector watching the raw signal for a drop below a fixed threshold will falsely declare the fault cleared at every one of those ordinary zero-crossings. The forward-looking envelope is used specifically for collapse detection, since it stays high right up until a real conducting burst has actually ended; the backward-looking envelope is used specifically for onset detection, since it rises at the first true sample of a burst rather than lagging behind it. Two more figures are computed once per waveform to make the envelope thresholds self-calibrating rather than fixed constants: a reference amplitude, taken from the upper tail of the signal's own magnitude distribution, and a noise floor, taken from the quietest blocks of the record. Every threshold the algorithm uses - what counts as "on," what counts as "off," how close to the noise floor a genuine residual conduction tail is allowed to sit - is derived from those two per-waveform figures rather than hardcoded, so the same algorithm calibrates itself to whatever amplitude and noise level a given capture actually has.

Onset detection happens in two stages: a coarse, high-confidence crossing of the "on" envelope threshold, deliberately guarded against a lone noise spike by also requiring a sustained forward-looking average above a lower bar; and a refinement stage that looks backward from that coarse point for genuine sub-threshold conduction that may have begun slightly earlier, close to a zero-crossing, below the coarse detector's own confidence level. Collapse detection works symmetrically in the other direction, searching forward from onset for the first point where the envelope stays below the "off" threshold for a full mains cycle - that persistence requirement is exactly what prevents an ordinary zero-crossing mid-fault from being mistaken for the actual end of conduction - and then walking the detected collapse point back to the nearest raw sample that actually crossed the threshold, since the smoothed envelope itself lags the true crossing by a small, characterizable amount.

Alongside the trip-time number itself, the algorithm computes a fixed set of sanity checks on every waveform: whether a real signal was present at all, whether there is any sign of current flowing before the fault was even supposed to begin (a K3-stuck-closed indicator), whether the captured record was even long enough to conclusively rule out a non-trip, whether the detected onset lines up with when the fault was actually commanded, and whether the detected collapse looks like a clean, final end of conduction rather than a temporary lull. All but two of these are logged as diagnostic information only and never change the computed verdict; the two exceptions - a real sign of pre-fault leakage current, and a record too short to trust a no-trip conclusion - are treated as serious enough to halt the test rig outright rather than simply being noted alongside a number. The design intent behind keeping the rest logged-only rather than verdict-changing is to preserve both the measurement and any doubt about it side by side, rather than letting an automatic sanity check quietly overrule a number a human reviewer might have judged differently.

### 3.3 Algorithm Evolution: V1 → V2 → V3

The analysis algorithm is treated as a versioned boundary rather than a single fixed implementation, and that distinction is deliberate: the raw waveform is the permanent record, and the algorithm applied to it is allowed to improve over time, provided every result it has ever produced remains tagged with the exact version of the algorithm that produced it and remains re-derivable from the stored waveform on demand. The system's own rule, stated in its own configuration text, is that an algorithm defect is fixed by introducing a new version and never by editing an existing one in place; a previous version's behavior, defect included, is preserved exactly as it was for historical replay, and a dedicated tool exists specifically to re-run a newer algorithm version against an old, archived waveform without re-running the physical test. This is not a theoretical safeguard - the system has already gone through three real versions, and the second version's real defect is the clearest demonstration of why the discipline exists.

The first version's weakness was in how it handled a record with no clearly quiet stretch of samples anywhere before the detected onset: rather than conclude nothing could be proven about the true start of conduction, it assumed conduction had been present from the very first sample of the record, which produced overly conservative behavior on some genuinely clean captures. The second version corrected that assumption and added a raw-sample check specifically aimed at detecting current flowing before the fault was ever supposed to begin - but in doing so introduced a new and more serious problem: its refined-onset logic could be dragged backward by a single noisy or quantization-limited sample crossing a very low residual threshold, with nothing in the algorithm confirming that sample represented real, sustained conduction rather than an isolated artifact. In practice, on real hardware, this was not a hypothetical failure mode. One real captured cycle had its onset dragged back roughly 123 milliseconds into what was actually silent pre-trigger recording, collapsing its apparent trip time to essentially zero and producing a passing verdict that should have been treated as suspect on its face; a second real cycle had its onset dragged back by a smaller but still material amount, inflating what was in fact a normal, passing trip time into a reading that crossed the pass/fail boundary and reported a fail. Both of those results were flagged by the algorithm's own downstream sanity checks as suspicious, but the sanity checks were not - and, by design, are still not - permitted to override the verdict itself, so the erroneous numbers stood as reported until a person inspected them.

The third and current version closes that gap by adding one further requirement before a refined onset is trusted: the raw samples at that point in the record must actually stay above the residual threshold for a short sustained run, not merely cross it once. If nothing near the refined candidate satisfies that stricter confirmation, the algorithm falls back to the coarser, already-trustworthy onset it found in its first stage rather than trusting an unconfirmed candidate. That fix was verified both by re-running it against the two real waveforms that had been affected and by a dedicated synthetic test built specifically to reproduce the scattered-noise mechanism that caused the original defect. The two earlier versions' results, including the two flawed real readings described above, remain stored and untouched in the historical record exactly as they were originally computed; only a version applied on demand through the replay tool reflects the correction.

### 3.4 Charging-Gate Detection via Machine Vision

Confirming that the EVSE has actually reached a charging state before a fault is injected is handled by a dedicated vision subsystem built around a single governing principle: it is allowed to grant permission for a cycle to proceed, and it is allowed to report a fault indication as secondary evidence, but it is never allowed to compute or influence a trip-time measurement, and a failure anywhere inside it is never allowed to bring the test rig itself down. Camera frames are converted to hue, saturation, and value and classified against a small set of expected LED colors within a fixed region of the frame corresponding to the EVSE's own status indicator; a frame in which two or more colors are simultaneously present is treated as evidence of the device's multi-color boot animation rather than any single steady state, and a frame that is neither clearly lit in a recognized color nor clearly dark is reported honestly as unknown rather than forced into the nearest guess.

Two separate decision layers are built on top of that per-frame classification, deliberately kept independent of each other rather than merged into one. The first is a slow, cautious temporal classifier that only declares an overall LED state - booting, ready, charging, faulted, off - once several consecutive few-second observation windows have agreed with each other; its purpose is to answer "what state is this LED actually in" with confidence, and it is used for diagnostics and for deciding how the system should respond if a cycle times out waiting for a charging state that never arrives. The second, entirely separate layer is the one that actually authorizes a fault injection to proceed: rather than requiring a sustained, unbroken green reading, it accumulates individual green-frame observations directly, tolerating the dark gaps a real flashing charging indicator naturally produces between flashes, and only grants once a minimum number of green observations have been seen spanning a minimum real elapsed time. That second requirement exists specifically to prevent a brief green frame appearing during the device's own boot animation - which cycles through every color, including green, on its way to a stable state - from ever being mistaken for genuine charging. An observation of red or blue, by contrast, immediately clears whatever green evidence had been accumulating, since either is a direct signal that the device is not in a charging state regardless of what was seen moments before. The two layers exist as separate systems because they answer genuinely different questions: one has to be slow and certain, and the other has to tolerate exactly the kind of intermittent, flashing signal the slow classifier is designed to look past.

If the camera itself becomes unreliable - a run of consecutive frames that cannot be read or decoded - the vision subsystem does not raise an error up into the rest of the rig. It logs the condition, records it as a degraded mode, waits a fixed interval, and returns control to the caller in a well-defined state; the test rig is built to treat a camera problem as a reason to wait and record a degraded flag, never as a reason to crash or to inject a fault without a valid charging-state confirmation.

### 3.5 Pass/Fail/No-Trip Criteria

Once a trip time has been computed, or determined not to exist at all, the verdict follows directly from where that number falls relative to the two configured limits, and from nothing else. A waveform in which no real collapse was ever found within the captured record - either because no signal was present to begin with, or because the fault current was still conducting at the end of the record - is reported as a no-trip, since the device did not clear the fault within the time the rig gave it to do so. A trip time at or above the no-trip limit is likewise reported as a no-trip rather than as an extremely slow pass or fail, with a small fixed margin applied only at that boundary and only in the direction that avoids ever under-reporting a genuine non-clearing device as merely slow. A trip time at or below the pass limit is reported as a pass, with no equivalent safety margin applied on that side of the boundary - the pass limit is evaluated at its literal configured value. Anything in between is reported as a fail.

One design choice here is deliberate and worth stating plainly: none of the sanity checks described in Section 3.2, including the ones that would themselves halt the rig on future cycles, are ever consulted when deciding the verdict of the cycle they were computed on. A verdict and the diagnostic doubts about it are recorded side by side rather than one silently overriding the other, on the reasoning that an automatic check quietly vetoing a number removes exactly the kind of visibility a careful reviewer needs when a result looks unusual - a discipline that, as Section 3.3 describes, is precisely what allowed two real flawed readings to be identified after the fact rather than simply disappearing into an automatically corrected number.

## 4. Commissioning and System Validation

### 4.1 Pre-Campaign Software Validation

The system was validated in a fixed, deliberately staged sequence, moving from pure software toward real hardware one small step at a time rather than assembling the whole rig and testing it as a unit on the first attempt. The earliest stage never touches hardware at all: the full automated test suite is run on the Raspberry Pi itself, followed by a full simulated campaign exercised entirely through the software's own simulated hardware drivers, including a deliberately induced crash partway through a run to confirm that resume behavior actually recovers cleanly rather than losing or duplicating a cycle. Only once that stage passes cleanly does hardware enter the picture, and even then only one subsystem at a time - a real-GPIO check that exercises the contactors without any real fault current present, a real-oscilloscope check that confirms connection, configuration, and arming without requiring an actual trigger event, and a camera calibration pass against real footage of the EVSE's own status LED. The hardware-abstraction-layer split described in Section 2.4 is what makes this staging possible at all: each subsystem's mode can be flipped from simulated to real independently in configuration, so a single real subsystem can be validated in isolation with everything else still simulated around it, rather than requiring every subsystem to be trustworthy simultaneously before anything real can be tested.

This staged approach caught real problems well before any hardware was at risk. Bringing up the Raspberry Pi itself surfaced a genuinely non-obvious defect: a required GPIO driver package was installed system-wide but invisible to the project's isolated Python virtual environment, causing the GPIO library to silently fall back to an experimental, less trustworthy backend rather than failing loudly - a failure mode that would have looked like ordinary success on a quick manual check and would not have been trustworthy across a multi-day endurance run. A second, unrelated surprise came from the Raspberry Pi's own temporary-file filesystem being far smaller than the project's own disk-space safety guard required, which caused every software-only test to halt on a disk-space fault despite tens of gigabytes of real free space being available elsewhere on the same machine - not a bug in the guard, but a mismatch between where it was checking and where temporary files were actually being written. Neither of these problems involved the electrical rig at all, and both were found and fixed before the software validation ladder ever reached real hardware.

### 4.2 The Oscilloscope No-Trigger Investigation

The most demanding commissioning effort by a wide margin was diagnosing why real, energized cycles were consistently failing to produce a scope acquisition at all. For an extended period, every real cycle armed the oscilloscope, closed the leakage-injection contactor, and simply never triggered - the scope stayed armed, the trace stayed flat, and the cycle timed out and halted, on a rig whose contactors, camera, and analysis logic had all already been separately confirmed working. Several genuine, real defects were found and fixed over the course of this investigation, and none of them turned out to be the actual cause.

The first candidate was a missing explicit trigger-mode command that, on this instrument, is required before edge-trigger parameters take effect at all; it was a real bug, worth fixing on its own merits, and it did not fix the no-trigger condition. The second candidate, found through code review rather than another live trial, was a command-ordering defect in which the oscilloscope's channel scale and offset were being sent before its probe attenuation ratio, causing every acquisition to be silently digitized against a stale probe ratio - a defect invisible to a settings readback, since the misapplied values still reported back as correct, and invisible to the software's own simulated oscilloscope, which had no equivalent physics to get wrong. This was also a real, worthwhile fix, and it also did not resolve the no-trigger condition when tried against real hardware. Ruling out both of those pushed the investigation toward the trigger comparator itself, which watches a physically separate signal path from what gets digitized, and toward a piece of instrumentation that had not been read before: the oscilloscope's own trigger-event register, which answers whether the trigger comparator has actually fired, independent of whether the acquisition subsystem ever reports a completed capture.

Building the diagnostic capability needed to read that register safely was itself a source of real incidents. An early version of the timeout-diagnostics capture wedged the oscilloscope's USB interface badly enough that only removing its AC power entirely for a full minute would recover it - neither a USB reconnect, a scope power cycle, nor a Raspberry Pi reboot was sufficient - which produced the governing rule that diagnostics of any kind must only ever run after the rig has been fully and completely de-energized, never while still live. A corrected version of that capability was then found to have its own serious flaw: a background-thread-based timeout meant to bound a slow query gave up waiting on the query without actually stopping it, and the abandoned background operation later collided with the main program thread and crashed the process outright. That was fixed by removing the threading approach entirely in favor of a synchronous, natively-bounded timeout enforced by the instrument driver itself, and by permanently marking any connection unusable the moment a single query on it fails, rather than trusting a connection whose internal state could no longer be verified.

With that instrumentation finally trustworthy, a diagnostic-only forced-trigger capability - explicitly and permanently walled off from ever being able to influence a real measurement - froze the very first direct evidence of the leakage waveform itself: a large bipolar transient, roughly two hundred volts peak to peak, captured while the trigger-event register confirmed the scope's own comparator had never fired naturally at all. That confirmed a genuine no-trigger condition, not a hypothesis, and it also produced one further wrong conclusion that had to be corrected in the tooling rather than just walked back once: an attempt to place that captured burst at a specific point in time relative to when the fault was commanded assumed a controller-side timestamp and the oscilloscope's own internal waveform clock could be compared directly, when nothing in the system actually synchronizes those two clocks to each other. The fix was structural rather than one-off - every subsequent claim about where something falls within a captured waveform is derived only from the waveform's own samples and its own scope-supplied time base, never from a controller-side timestamp, a principle that also shaped the trip-time algorithm described in Section 3.1.

The actual root cause, once found, had nothing to do with the electrical signal path at all. A single status flag inside the forced-trigger diagnostic logic was being used to mean two different things at once - that a diagnostic checkpoint had run, and that a trigger had actually been forced - and on exactly the branch where a real, natural trigger was correctly detected and forcing was correctly skipped, the surrounding polling loop still permanently stopped checking for a real, successful acquisition regardless. In effect, some fraction of the cycles this entire investigation was built around may well have triggered successfully on real hardware and been silently misreported as failures by the diagnostic tooling itself, not by any defect in the electrical measurement path. Once that state-tracking defect was corrected, natural triggering became repeatable. The lesson carried forward from this investigation, stated at the time in almost exactly these words, is to audit a system's own control-state flags before changing proven electrical settings in response to a symptom - four separate, individually real hardware-adjacent defects were found and fixed along the way, and the actual cause was in none of them.

### 4.3 Vision System Commissioning

The vision subsystem went through two real redesigns before it reached its current, validated behavior, on top of a number of smaller mechanical issues resolved earlier - the camera needing several seconds of active frame reads before its exposure and gain settle, manual exposure resetting to automatic on every USB reconnect and needing to be explicitly reapplied, and a calibrated region of interest that, for a period, was correctly produced by the calibration tooling but never actually wired into the runtime path the real sequencer used, so a properly calibrated ROI and the ROI the software was actually reading from were two different things until that gap was closed.

The first substantive defect was found through live commissioning rather than a bench test: a real cycle halted on a stuck-booting vision-gate timeout while an operator was directly watching the EVSE's LED flash green, indicating the classifier itself was wrong, not merely slow. Deliberately reproducing the condition with a synthetic noisy frame sequence confirmed the mechanism - the temporal window classifier described in Section 3.4 could be tipped from a correct reading into a false booting verdict by as few as two stray-colored frames out of an approximately forty-five-frame observation window, a real risk from ordinary camera noise or glare that clean synthetic test fixtures had never exercised. The fix did not alter that classifier at all, since it remains correct and useful for its own purpose of reporting overall LED state; instead, the actual charging-gate authorization was moved onto the entirely separate, flash-tolerant policy described in Section 3.4, built specifically because the existing classifier's slow, whole-window-agreement design was the wrong tool for recognizing a genuinely flashing indicator.

That fix immediately exposed a second, smaller defect in the fix itself: the new policy's first version accepted as few as three green frames within a two-second window, which the EVSE's own multi-color boot animation could satisfy on its own well before the device was actually charging, risking a fault injection against a unit that only appeared to be ready. Requiring the accumulated green evidence to span at least three and a half real seconds within a six-second window - validated against the real shape of the boot animation rather than a synthetic approximation - closed that gap, along with two smaller refinements: clearing accumulated evidence on either a red or a blue observation rather than red alone, and treating a dark or unreadable frame between flashes as neutral rather than as a reason to discard evidence already accumulated, since a genuinely flashing indicator necessarily produces dark frames between its flashes. The resulting gate typically takes on the order of forty-five to fifty seconds to grant on a real device, which was itself briefly suspected as a new defect before being confirmed as simply the real combination of the EVSE's own boot sequence and the now-required sustained-green qualification. The corrected policy was validated for real by physically disabling the leakage-injection contactor's own coil supply and running the real camera and real contactors against a simulated oscilloscope - a deliberate pattern that produces a synthetic pass result that must never be mistaken for genuine electrical trip evidence, and the configuration that enables it is kept out of the project's own version control specifically so it cannot be run by accident.

### 4.4 Auto-Retry and Equipment-Refresh Resilience

Two real incidents on large unattended runs, arriving from two different directions, exposed the same underlying gap: for most of the system's development, any halt at all - a scope timeout, an unexpected software exception, a disk-space fault, a genuine device failure - ended the campaign outright and left it idle until a person noticed an alert and manually resumed it. The first incident halted a real, large campaign with an unexpected exception whose original diagnostic detail did not survive a subsequent loss of connectivity to the Pi, since the system's own logging was not, at the time, configured to persist across a reboot; what was left was a plausible explanation from code review rather than a confirmed one - a timing-boundary defect in how long the acquisition-wait loop computed its own remaining budget, capable of occasionally handing the instrument driver a negative or zero timeout it explicitly rejects. That defect was fixed, but the more durable outcome was structural: every unexpected controller-level failure now writes its own full diagnostic record - exception detail, a complete state-transition history, timestamps - directly to durable per-cycle storage as part of the normal halt path, deliberately independent of whether the system's own log stream survives whatever happens next.

The second, broader realization was that ending the entire campaign on every halt was itself the real source of a multi-day run appearing to "keep crashing," when in many cases nothing about the rig was actually wrong. Resolving that required a genuine design tension to be worked through deliberately rather than defaulted past: halts are sticky by design specifically so a device that has already failed a real test is never silently re-tested without a human being aware a halt occurred, and an indiscriminate auto-retry-everything policy would have quietly defeated that safeguard. The resolution treats halt categories asymmetrically. A no-trip verdict - a genuine failure of the device this rig exists to test for - gets a short leash of three consecutive occurrences before a human is required, while every other kind of halt not tied to a device verdict gets a longer leash of five, on the reasoning that those are substantially more likely to be transient rig or software issues than evidence the device under test has actually failed. Either streak resets the moment a cycle genuinely completes.

Building that behavior correctly surfaced two further real defects, both caught by writing a regression test rather than by inspection alone. An early version of the "did progress actually happen" check counted any completed cycle, including a no-trip, as progress - which would have silently reset the no-trip streak's own counter the instant a no-trip occurred, defeating the entire reason that streak exists to be tighter than the others; it was corrected to check whether more than one cycle appears within a single continuous run attempt, which only a genuine completion can produce. Separately, retrying after certain halts risked colliding with the charging-gate authorization token described in Section 2.2, which is deliberately single-use per cycle: a halt that occurred after the gate had already been legitimately consumed for that cycle, but before a result was ever committed, could cause a naive retry to attempt reusing a token that had already done its job, which the system correctly refuses. The fix was to always advance past whichever cycle number was actually attempted, committed or not, so a retry is always addressed to a fresh cycle rather than one whose gate token has already been spent.

A separate incident, arriving from the hardware side rather than the software side, exposed a related gap: the camera's operating-system device path re-enumerated to a different device index mid-campaign following a real USB reconnect event, and several consecutive cycles reported the camera unavailable before the vision gate could even begin its normal wait, since the system had been pointed at a fixed numeric index that no longer corresponded to the camera at all. Two changes addressed this together. First, the camera's configured device path was changed from a raw numeric index to a stable path created by a dedicated udev rule keyed to the camera's own USB serial number, so which numeric index the kernel happens to assign it on a given boot no longer matters. Second, the system gained the ability to proactively disconnect and reconnect both the camera and the oscilloscope on its own - on a fixed schedule every fifty cycles as a general precaution against gradual USB or driver degradation over a multi-day run, and reactively, immediately, after three consecutive camera-unavailable cycles rather than waiting for the next scheduled interval. Both triggers fire strictly before mains are ever closed for the cycle they apply to, and both are best-effort; neither can recover an oscilloscope connection that has already been permanently marked unusable following an earlier unrecoverable diagnostics failure, since that flag is set once, deliberately, and never cleared automatically.

### 4.5 Known Limitations Entering the Campaign

A number of items were, and remain, known and open rather than hidden going into the system's first large endurance campaign, and are stated here plainly rather than glossed over. The software tracks only the contactor state it has itself commanded; there is no auxiliary-contact or voltage feedback confirming a contactor physically reached the state it was told to reach, and this is a deliberately deferred gap rather than an oversight. Protective-earth continuity is not independently, continuously monitored by any isolated sensing path; a protective-earth fault would only be inferred indirectly, after the fact, through its downstream consequences. A small number of real waveforms have been observed to produce a trip-time reading of exactly zero on the very first cycle of a run, for a reason not yet root-caused; the current handling is to treat that specific behavior as unresolved and to exclude it from performance interpretation rather than to accept it as a genuine near-instantaneous trip. The measurement endpoint definition itself, as stated in Section 3.1, remains the project's own provisional reading of UL 2231-2 §23.3.1 pending confirmation against the standard's published text. No campaign-level statistical acceptance criterion - what pass rate, across how many cycles, would constitute a satisfactory result - has been defined anywhere in the software, and that omission is deliberate rather than an open task: that judgment is reserved for a human, made deliberately and offline, and this system does not infer or default to one. Finally, the auto-retry and equipment-refresh behaviors described in Section 4.4 were, at the point the system entered its first large campaign, validated against realistic simulated failure conditions and passing their own regression tests, but neither had yet been proven against a live repeat of the exact real-hardware incident that motivated it in the first place.

## 5. Conclusion

Project A.M.P.E.R.E. was built to close a specific gap: a real reliability claim about a CCID's trip-time behavior requires exercising it across far more cycles than a person can safely and precisely operate by hand, and no part of that gap can be closed by simply running faster - it has to be closed by a system that injects a fault, captures the result with enough timing precision to distinguish a pass from a fail, and does all of it safely and unattended, cycle after cycle, for as long as the campaign requires. The design choices described in this report all trace back to that one requirement in one way or another: contactors that fail safe rather than merely being commanded safe, an analysis algorithm built around a permanent raw record specifically so that a future improvement never requires repeating a physical test, a vision system that is allowed to gate a cycle but never allowed to touch a measurement, and a persistence layer built around the assumption that the process running it will, eventually, crash.

None of that was true by construction alone. Section 4 describes real defects found in real ways - a scope that would not trigger for reasons that turned out to have nothing to do with the scope, a vision classifier fooled by ordinary camera noise, a retry mechanism that had to be built twice to avoid quietly defeating its own purpose - and each of those investigations changed the system in a way that is now part of its permanent design, not a patch layered on top of it. The system that results is not presented here as flawless; Section 4.5 states plainly what remained open and unresolved at the point this report was written, and that list is meant to be read as honestly as the rest of this document, not as an afterthought.

What this report has established is that the system as built - its electrical safety design, its measurement methodology, and the validation process it went through - was ready to be trusted with a large, unattended endurance campaign. Whether it actually performed that way at scale, what the resulting trip-time data looks like, and what incidents occurred over the course of thousands of real cycles, is not something this report can answer in the abstract. That is the subject of Addendum I.

## Appendix B. Glossary

**CCID** — Charge Circuit Interrupting Device; the ground-fault protective device under test.
**EVSE** — Electric Vehicle Supply Equipment; the charging unit the CCID protects and the rig energizes.
**CP** — Control Pilot; the EVSE-to-vehicle communication signal that the spoof device emulates.
**Spoof / EV simulator** — A static, always-on device presenting a fake vehicle presence to the EVSE, used so a full charge cycle can be exercised without a physical vehicle connected.
**K1 / K2 / K3** — The three contactors: K1 and K2 switch the L1 and L2 mains legs; K3 switches the leakage-injection path that produces the fault the CCID must clear.
**DUT** — Device Under Test; in this system, the CCID.
**HAL** — Hardware Abstraction Layer; the interchangeable real/simulated driver interface described in Section 2.4.
**ROI** — Region of Interest; the fixed pixel rectangle within a camera frame that the vision system actually classifies, rather than the full frame.
**Trip** — The CCID successfully clearing the injected fault within the allowed time window.
**No-trip** — The CCID failing to clear the fault within the allowed window; the only device condition that halts a run outright.
**Sticky halt** — The system's design property that a halted run remains halted across restarts until a human explicitly overrides it, rather than silently resuming.
**t0 / t_end** — The resolved onset and end instants of a fault event, whose difference is the reported trip time (Section 3.1).

## Appendix C. Hardware As-Built Reference

**Contactors and drivers.** Three independently driven single-pole contactors: K1 (L1 mains, GPIO17 / physical pin 11), K2 (L2 mains, GPIO27 / physical pin 13), K3 (leakage injection, GPIO22 / physical pin 15). Each is switched by its own ZX-517 dual-MOSFET opto-driver board from its own isolated 12 VDC, 2 A supply. The ZX-517 boards are non-isolated and have no onboard flyback diode; each coil has an external 1N5404 flyback diode (cathode to `OUT+`, anode to `OUT-`). Each MOSFET gate input carries a pulldown resistor of approximately 10 kΩ. All three driver boards share a single ground bond to the Raspberry Pi at one star point. GPIO outputs are active-high, initialized inactive.

**Coil specification.** 12 VDC nominal, approximately 0.462 A, approximately 26 Ω coil resistance. Operate voltage at or below 9 VDC; maximum rated voltage 13.2 VDC; release voltage at or above 1.2 VDC.

**Oscilloscope.** Keysight MSO-X 2014A, firmware version 02.43.2018020635, connected over USB and driven via PyVISA with the PyVISA-py backend and PyUSB transport (no ARM-native VISA runtime exists for this platform). Trigger: positive edge, channel 1 source, +20 V level, DC trigger coupling, noise-reject disabled (confirmed unsupported on this specific unit). Acquisition: RAW waveform mode, BYTE format, one-million-point record, 50 ms/div, centered timebase.

**Probe.** 10:1 passive attenuation, 300 V CAT II rating.

**Camera.** Logitech C270 USB webcam, YUYV 640×480 capture format, manual exposure locked (auto exposure and auto white balance both disabled/worked around), aimed at the EVSE's status LED, resolved via a udev rule keyed to the camera's USB serial number to a stable `/dev/ccid_camera` symlink.

**Controller.** Raspberry Pi, GPIO driven via `gpiozero` on the `lgpio` backend.
